This is a real banknote. One hundred trillion dollars.

It was issued in Zimbabwe in January two thousand nine. On the day it came out, it was worth about thirty US dollars.

A few months earlier, prices in Zimbabwe were doubling roughly every twenty-five hours.

So if a government can print money, why doesn't every country just print as much as it wants? Why not print enough to make everyone rich?

To answer that, let's shrink the economy down to something tiny.

Imagine an island. There are ten loaves of bread, and ten dollars. Nothing else to buy. So each loaf costs one dollar.

Now the island's government decides everyone should be richer. It prints ten more dollars and hands them out.

Everyone has twice as much money. But there are still only ten loaves of bread.

So what happens? Bakers notice that people can pay more, and the price of a loaf moves toward two dollars.

Nobody got richer. The money doubled, the bread didn't, and each dollar now buys half as much.

That's inflation at its simplest: more money chasing the same amount of stuff.

Money isn't wealth. Bread is wealth. Houses, phones, doctors' time, that's wealth. Money is just a claim on those things. Printing more claims doesn't create more bread.

In Zimbabwe, the government printed more and more money to pay its bills, while the economy was producing less and less. More money, fewer goods.

At the peak, in November two thousand eight, prices were rising seventy-nine billion percent a month.

Think about what that means. If you got paid in the morning, your salary was worth noticeably less by the evening. Saving was pointless. The only smart move was to spend your money the moment you got it, which pushed prices up even faster.

And in April two thousand nine, the country stopped using its own currency altogether. People switched to US dollars.

But wait. The United States printed a lot of money too.

In February twenty twenty, the US money supply was about fifteen and a half trillion dollars. Two years later, it was almost twenty-two trillion. That's an increase of about forty percent.

So why didn't America become Zimbabwe?

Inflation did come. In June twenty twenty-two, US prices were up nine point one percent in a year, the biggest jump in forty years. That hurt. But it wasn't hyperinflation.

There are four big reasons.

First, timing. In twenty twenty, the economy was shut down. People were stuck at home, saving instead of spending. A lot of that new money sat still.

Second, the whole world wants dollars. About fifty-seven percent of all foreign currency reserves held by central banks are in US dollars. When you print a currency the whole world uses, the extra money spreads much further.

Third, the US borrows in its own currency. Many countries borrow in dollars. They can print their own money, but they can't print dollars. When their currency falls, their debts get bigger.

And fourth, trust. The US central bank is independent from the government, and when inflation rose, it raised interest rates hard to pull it back down. People believed it would.

Remember the last video? Most money is created by banks when they lend. And higher rates slow that down. That's the brake.

So is printing money always bad? Not exactly. Most central banks actually aim for a little inflation, around two percent a year. A slowly growing money supply keeps up with a growing economy. The danger isn't printing. It's printing much faster than the economy can grow.

So who decides how much money to print? In most countries today, it's not the president or the parliament. It's an independent central bank.

That's on purpose. Politicians always have a reason to print more: an election, a war, a crisis. Central banks were separated from them precisely so that the printing press isn't one vote away.

When that separation breaks, and a government uses its central bank to pay its bills, you get stories like Zimbabwe.

So, why can't countries just print as much as they want?

Because money only works if it's worth something. Print too much, and every dollar buys less, until people stop trusting it at all.

You can print money. You can't print bread.
