## Verified numbers (all calculated with monthly compounding, $100 deposited at the end of each month)

| Annual return | 10 years | 20 years | 30 years | 40 years |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0% (under the mattress) | $12,000 | $24,000 | $36,000 | $48,000 |
| 4% | $14,725 | $36,677 | $69,405 | $118,196 |
| 7% (stocks, after inflation) | $17,308 | $52,093 | $121,997 | $262,481 |
| 10% (stocks, before inflation) | $20,484 | $75,937 | $226,049 | $632,408 |

Key facts used in the script:
- Total deposited over 40 years: $48,000
- Growth at 7% over 40 years: $214,481, which is about 82% of the final balance
- Money added in each decade at 7%: $17,308, then $34,784, then $69,904, then $140,484
- The last decade adds more than the first three decades combined ($140,484 vs $121,996)
- Year 11 at 7%: the growth earned that year ($1,290) is larger than the $1,200 deposited that year
- Starting 10 years late (30 years at 7%): $121,997, less than half
- To catch up in 30 years you would need about $215 a month instead of $100

Source for the return assumption: the S&P 500 averaged about 10.47% a year from 1926 to 2026 with dividends reinvested, and about 7.29% a year after inflation (officialdata.org). The script uses 7% so every dollar figure is in today's money.

## Script and scenes

Visual notes are for the animation. Voiceover (VO) is what you record. Rewrite any line so it sounds like you.

### Scene 1: Hook (0:00 to 0:15)
VO: If you put away 100 dollars a month, every month, for 40 years, how much would you end up with? Most people guess somewhere around 50 thousand. The real answer is more than five times that. And the reason why is one of the most important ideas in all of money.
Visual: Black navy screen. "$100" fades in, small. A month counter ticks fast. A big question mark. Then "$262,481" slams in large, amber underline. Hold, then cut to logo intro.

### Scene 2: Logo intro (0:15 to 0:18)
Visual: Logo intro component.

### Scene 3: The rules (0:18 to 0:45)
VO: Let's set the rules. 100 dollars a month. That's about 3 dollars a day, roughly the price of a coffee. You start at 25, and you keep going until 65. No raises, no bonuses, no lucky stock picks. Just 100 dollars, 480 times in a row.
Visual: Three cards appear one by one: "$100 / month", "Age 25 to 65", "480 deposits". A small coffee cup icon next to "$3.29 a day". Cards slide aside.

### Scene 4: The mattress baseline (0:45 to 1:15)
VO: First, the boring version. You hide the money under your mattress. After 40 years you'd have exactly what you put in: 48 thousand dollars. Not bad. But also, not more. Every dollar you have is a dollar you saved. Keep that number in mind, because it's the floor for everything that follows.
Visual: A single cream bar grows step by step, one segment per year, 40 segments, counter reaches "$48,000". Label: "What you put in". The bar dims to a faint outline and stays on screen as a reference.

### Scene 5: What compounding actually is (1:15 to 2:15)
VO: Now let's do something different. Instead of the mattress, you invest. Imagine your money grows 7 percent a year. In year one, you put in 1,200 dollars, and it earns a little: about 40 dollars. Barely noticeable. But here's the trick. In year two, you don't just earn on your new deposits. You also earn on last year's growth. Your growth starts earning its own growth. That's compounding. Money making money, and then that money making more money. It feels slow at first. Then it doesn't.
Visual: One coin-like circle labeled "$1,200". A tiny amber sliver appears next to it: "+$40". Year 2: a new $1,200 circle, and small amber slivers appear on BOTH previous circle and previous sliver. Arrow loops: "growth earns growth". Simple, slow, clear.

### Scene 6: Why 7 percent (2:15 to 2:55)
VO: Why 7 percent? Over the last hundred years, the US stock market, measured by the S&P 500, returned about 10 percent a year on average. But prices also went up over that time, so after inflation, the real return was closer to 7. We'll use 7, so every number you see in this video is in today's dollars. It's an average, not a promise. Some years the market dropped 30 or 40 percent. We'll come back to that.
Visual: A long faint line chart of 100 years, jagged. Big label "~10% a year". A second label slides under it: "~7% after inflation", in amber. Lower third: "Source: officialdata.org, S&P 500 1926 to 2026".

### Scene 7: The curve (2:55 to 4:00)
VO: So here's what happens. On the bottom, the mattress: a straight line, 48 thousand dollars. A savings account at 4 percent does better, ending around 118 thousand. And at 7 percent? For the first decade, the lines barely separate. After 10 years you'd have about 17 thousand, only 5 thousand more than the mattress. Most people quit right here, because it looks like nothing is happening. Then the line starts to bend. 20 years: 52 thousand. 30 years: 122 thousand. And at 40 years: 262,481 dollars.
Visual: Line chart, x axis 0 to 40 years, y axis $0 to $300K. Three lines draw left to right in sync with VO: mattress (faint cream, straight), 4% (teal), 7% (amber, thick). Dots and value labels pop at 10, 20, 30, 40 years on the amber line. Final number counts up to "$262,481".

### Scene 8: Decade by decade (4:00 to 5:00)
VO: Look at it decade by decade. In your first 10 years, your account grows by 17 thousand dollars. In the second decade, 35 thousand. In the third, 70 thousand. And in the final decade, 140 thousand. That last decade alone adds more money than the first 30 years combined. Same 100 dollars a month the whole time. The only thing that changed was time.
Visual: Four vertical bars appear one at a time: $17K, $35K, $70K, $140K. Each roughly doubles the last. The last bar is amber. Then the first three bars stack on top of each other next to the fourth, and the stack is still shorter. Label: "Last 10 years > first 30 years".

### Scene 9: The tipping point (5:00 to 5:40)
VO: There's a moment in this story that's worth noticing. In year 11, something flips. That year, your investments earn about 1,290 dollars on their own. That's more than the 1,200 dollars you put in. From that point on, your money is working harder than you are. And every year after, the gap gets wider.
Visual: Two horizontal bars per year, side by side, years 1 to 40 scrolling: cream "you deposited" stays fixed at $1,200, amber "growth earned" grows. At year 11 the amber bar passes the cream one, freeze frame, label "Year 11: your money out-earns you". Then fast-forward to year 40, where the amber bar is many times longer.

### Scene 10: The cost of waiting (5:40 to 6:45)
VO: Now let's change one thing. Same 100 dollars, same 7 percent. But you start at 35 instead of 25. You only lose 10 years. You'd think you'd end up with about three quarters of the money. You don't. You end up with 122 thousand. Less than half. Those 10 years you skipped weren't at the start of the curve. They were at the end, where the biggest growth happens. To catch up, starting at 35, you'd need to invest about 215 dollars a month. More than double. Waiting 10 years doesn't cost you 10 years of deposits. It costs you the best decade.
Visual: Two amber curves on the same chart, one starting at year 0 and one starting at year 10 (both ending at age 65). The gap between them at the end is highlighted and labeled "$140,484 difference". Then a split card: "Start at 25: $100/mo" vs "Start at 35: $215/mo" for the same result.

### Scene 11: Where the money really came from (6:45 to 7:30)
VO: Back to our 262 thousand dollars. How much of it did you actually put in? 48 thousand. That's it. The other 214 thousand, about 82 percent, came from growth. Your own deposits are less than one fifth of the final pile. That's the sum of parts: small pieces, repeated, that add up to something far bigger than they look.
Visual: A large circle representing $262,481 splits into parts. A small cream slice (18%) labeled "You: $48,000". The large amber remainder labeled "Growth: $214,481". The amber part separates slightly outward, echoing the channel logo.

### Scene 12: The fine print (7:30 to 8:20)
VO: A few honest caveats. 7 percent is a long term average, not a guarantee. Real markets don't climb in a smooth line. They crash, sometimes hard, and the people who end up with these numbers are the ones who kept investing through the crashes instead of selling. Fees matter too: a fund charging 1 percent a year would cut your result by tens of thousands of dollars over 40 years. And of course, this is education, not financial advice. Your situation is your own.
Visual: The smooth amber curve morphs into a jagged real-world-looking path with visible drops, but ending in the same place. Labels at dips: "Crash", "Crash". Then a small card: "Fees: 1% a year = less growth". Then a small line: "Not financial advice".

### Scene 13: Takeaway and end card (8:20 to 9:00)
VO: So, what does 100 dollars a month become in 40 years? At the historical average, more than a quarter of a million dollars in today's money. Not because of a big salary or a perfect investment. Because of time. The amount matters less than you think. The start date matters more than you think. If you want to see how the money in your bank account is created in the first place, that's the next video. See you there.

## Description draft for this video

What happens if you invest $100 a month for 40 years? We run the real numbers, visualized step by step: compounding, the decade-by-decade breakdown, the year your money starts out-earning you, and what waiting 10 years really costs.

All figures use monthly compounding and a 7% annual return after inflation, based on the long-run average of the S&P 500. Past returns do not guarantee future results.

Sources:
S&P 500 returns 1926 to 2026, nominal and inflation-adjusted: officialdata.org/us/stocks/s-p-500/1926

This video is for education only and is not financial advice.
